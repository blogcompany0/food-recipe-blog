My first sourdough loaf looked like a frisbee. Flat, dense, and about as appetizing as a hockey puck. I almost gave up right there. But something about the process — the slow fermentation, the way the dough transforms overnight, the smell of it baking — kept pulling me back. Six months and probably thirty loaves later, I was turning out bread that rivaled the $8 loaves at the farmers market.

Sourdough has this reputation for being fussy and complicated. And sure, if you dive into the deep end of baking forums, you'll find people debating hydration percentages and crumb structure like it's a doctoral thesis. But the actual process? Flour, water, salt, time. That's it. People have been making this bread for thousands of years without thermometers or kitchen scales.

Here's my beginner-friendly sourdough bread recipe — no experience required.

## First Things First: Your Sourdough Starter

Before you can bake, you need a starter. This is just a mixture of flour and water that captures wild yeast from the environment. It's what makes sourdough rise without commercial yeast, and it's what gives the bread its signature tang.

**To build a starter from scratch:**

Day 1: Mix 50g whole wheat flour with 50g lukewarm water in a clean jar. Stir well, cover loosely, and leave at room temperature.

Day 2-6: Every 24 hours, discard half the mixture and feed it with 50g all-purpose flour and 50g water. Stir, cover, repeat.

By day 5 or 6, you should see consistent bubbling and a pleasant sour smell. The mixture should roughly double in size within 4-8 hours of feeding. That's when you know it's ready.

A few things that tripped me up early on:

**Temperature matters more than timing.** In a warm kitchen (75-80°F), your starter will be active and bubbly within 4-6 hours of feeding. In a cold kitchen, it might take 10-12 hours. Neither is wrong — you're just working on different schedules.

**Whole wheat flour jumpstarts things.** [King Arthur Baking explains](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/learn/guides/sourdough) that whole wheat flour contains more wild yeast and bacteria than white flour, which is why I recommend starting with it. Once your starter is established, you can switch to all-purpose for maintenance.

**Don't panic if it smells weird.** Around day 2-3, your starter might smell like nail polish remover or old cheese. That's normal — bad bacteria are competing with the good stuff. Keep feeding it. The beneficial lactobacillus will win out by day 5 or so.

## The Recipe: Your First Loaf

Once your starter is reliably doubling within 6-8 hours of feeding, you're ready to bake. This recipe is deliberately simple — no fancy techniques, no special equipment beyond a Dutch oven.

**Ingredients:**
- 100g active sourdough starter (fed 4-6 hours before)
- 375g lukewarm water
- 500g bread flour (all-purpose works too, just slightly less chewy)
- 10g salt

**That's the whole ingredient list.** Four things. The magic is in the process.

### Step 1: Mix (10 minutes active, 30 minutes rest)

Pour the water into a large bowl. Add the starter and stir until it dissolves — it doesn't have to be perfectly smooth. Add the flour and mix with your hands or a wooden spoon until no dry flour remains. It'll look shaggy and rough. That's perfect.

Cover the bowl and let it rest for 30 minutes. This is called autolyse, and it lets the flour fully hydrate. When you come back, the dough will already feel smoother and more cohesive. You haven't done anything — the flour did the work for you.

Now sprinkle the salt over the dough and squeeze it in with your hands. Fold the dough over itself a few times until the salt is incorporated. It'll feel a bit tighter and less sticky.

### Step 2: Bulk Fermentation (4-8 hours)

This is where patience comes in. Leave the dough covered at room temperature. Every 30-45 minutes for the first 2 hours, do a set of stretch and folds: wet your hand, grab one side of the dough, stretch it up, and fold it over the center. Rotate the bowl 90 degrees and repeat. Four folds per set.

These folds build strength in the dough without kneading. After 3-4 sets, you'll notice the dough becoming smoother, more elastic, and holding its shape better.

After the folds, leave the dough alone. Depending on your kitchen temperature, bulk fermentation takes 4-8 hours total. You're looking for the dough to increase in volume by about 50-75% — not quite doubled. It should feel airy and jiggly when you shake the bowl, with visible bubbles on the surface and sides.

### Step 3: Shape and Cold Proof (Overnight)

Flour your counter generously. Gently turn the dough out — don't punch it down or deflate it. Using a bench scraper or your hands, shape it into a round by tucking the edges underneath to create surface tension on top.

Let it rest for 20 minutes (this is called a bench rest), then shape it again: flip it smooth-side down, fold the edges into the center like an envelope, then flip it seam-side up into a floured bowl or banneton.

Cover with plastic wrap or a damp towel and put it in the fridge overnight — at least 8 hours, up to 24. This cold proof slows fermentation, develops flavor, and makes the dough much easier to score and handle before baking.

### Step 4: Bake (45-50 minutes)

Place your Dutch oven (lid on) in the oven and preheat to 450°F (230°C). Let it heat for at least 30 minutes — you want that pot screaming hot.

Carefully take the pot out. Turn your dough onto a piece of parchment paper, score the top with a sharp knife or razor blade (a single slash down the center works great for your first loaf), and lower it into the pot using the parchment as a sling.

Put the lid on and bake for 30 minutes. The lid traps steam, which is what gives sourdough its signature crispy, blistered crust. After 30 minutes, remove the lid and bake for another 15-20 minutes until the crust is deep golden brown — darker than you think. A pale loaf is an underbaked loaf.

Pull it out and — this is the hardest part — let it cool for at least an hour before cutting. The bread is still cooking inside from residual heat. Cutting too early gives you a gummy interior. I know it's torture. Put on a podcast. Go for a walk. The bread will be there when you get back.

## Troubleshooting Your First Few Loaves

**Dense, heavy crumb:** Your starter probably wasn't active enough, or you didn't ferment long enough. Make sure your starter passes the float test (a spoonful floats in water) before using it.

**Flat loaf that spreads sideways:** This usually means over-fermentation. The dough used up all its gas before it hit the oven. Try a shorter bulk ferment or a cooler spot in your kitchen.

**Gummy interior:** You either cut it too soon (wait a full hour!) or underbaked it. Don't be afraid of a dark crust — that's flavor.

**Bland taste:** Longer cold proof = more tang. Try leaving it in the fridge for 18-24 hours instead of the minimum 8.

## Maintaining Your Starter Long-Term

Once you're baking regularly, your starter becomes part of the household. I keep mine in the fridge and feed it once a week. The night before I want to bake, I pull it out, feed it, and let it come to room temperature overnight. By morning, it's bubbly and ready to go.

If you're not baking for a while, your starter can survive weeks in the fridge without feeding. It might develop a dark liquid on top (called hooch) — that's just alcohol from the fermentation. Stir it back in or pour it off, feed the starter, and it'll bounce back within a day or two.

Your twentieth will be something you're genuinely proud of. And somewhere along the way, the process stops feeling like a sourdough bread recipe you're following and starts feeling like something you just know how to do.
