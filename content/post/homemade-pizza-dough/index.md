I spent an embarrassing amount of money on delivery pizza before I realized I could make better homemade pizza dough at home with five ingredients and ten minutes of effort. Not "pretty good for homemade" dough — genuinely better than most pizzerias in my neighborhood.

The turning point was understanding that great pizza dough isn't about technique or fancy equipment. It's about time. The best doughs ferment slowly, developing flavor and texture that no amount of kneading can replicate. My recipe takes 10 minutes of hands-on work, then the fridge does the rest overnight. You wake up to dough that's bubbly, extensible, and ready to become the best pizza you've ever made at home.

## Why Homemade Beats Store-Bought

Store-bought pizza dough — the kind in tubes or plastic bags at the grocery store — is designed for shelf stability, not flavor. It's made with dough conditioners, preservatives, and usually too much sugar. It works in a pinch, but it tastes like what it is: a compromise.

Homemade dough has exactly five ingredients: flour, water, salt, yeast, and olive oil. That's it. The flavor comes from fermentation — the slow process of yeast eating sugars and producing carbon dioxide (for bubbles) and organic acids (for flavor). [Serious Eats' pizza lab](https://www.seriouseats.com/the-pizza-lab-how-to-make-great-new-york-style-pizza) goes deep on the science, but the short version is: more time = more flavor.

## The Recipe

This makes enough dough for three 12-inch pizzas. I usually make the full batch and freeze what I don't use.

**Ingredients:**
- 500g bread flour (all-purpose works, but bread flour gives you more chew)
- 325g lukewarm water (65% hydration — this is the sweet spot for home ovens)
- 10g salt
- 3g instant yeast (about 1 teaspoon)
- 15g olive oil (about 1 tablespoon)

A note on flour: [bread flour has higher protein content](https://www.pizzamaking.com/flour-information.html) than all-purpose (12-14% vs 10-12%), which means more gluten development, which means a chewier, more structured crust. If you only have all-purpose, it'll still make good pizza — just slightly more tender and less chewy.

And on yeast: I use instant yeast because you can mix it directly into the flour without activating it in water first. [King Arthur Baking's yeast guide](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2020/04/01/yeast-for-baking) explains the differences between yeast types, but for this recipe, instant or active dry both work. If using active dry, dissolve it in the water first and wait 5 minutes until it foams.

### Step 1: Mix (5 minutes)

Combine the flour, salt, and yeast in a large bowl. Add the water and olive oil. Stir with a wooden spoon or your hand until a shaggy dough forms — no dry flour should remain, but it doesn't need to be smooth. It'll look rough and messy. That's fine.

That's the entire mixing process. No kneading required. Time and hydration will develop the gluten for you.

### Step 2: Bulk Rise (1-2 hours at room temperature)

Cover the bowl with plastic wrap or a damp towel. Let it sit at room temperature for 1-2 hours. During this time, do two sets of stretch and folds — one at the 30-minute mark and one at the 60-minute mark.

For each set: wet your hand, grab one side of the dough, stretch it up, and fold it over the center. Rotate the bowl 90 degrees and repeat. Four folds per set. This builds structure without kneading and takes about 30 seconds.

After the second set of folds, the dough should feel noticeably smoother and more elastic. It won't have doubled in size yet, and that's okay — the real magic happens in the fridge.

### Step 3: Divide and Cold Ferment (Overnight to 72 hours)

Turn the dough onto a lightly floured surface. Divide into three equal pieces (about 280g each). Shape each piece into a tight ball by tucking the edges underneath and rotating it on the counter with cupped hands.

Place each ball in a lightly oiled container or on a lightly oiled sheet pan, cover tightly with plastic wrap, and refrigerate.

Here's where patience pays off. The dough is usable after 24 hours, good after 48, and exceptional after 72. The cold slows the yeast down but doesn't stop it — the dough continues to ferment slowly, developing complex flavors that a quick-rise dough simply can't match. After 48-72 hours, you'll notice the dough has a slightly tangy, almost sourdough-like quality.

### Step 4: Shape

Pull a dough ball from the fridge 1-2 hours before you want to bake. Let it come to room temperature — cold dough is stiff and tears easily.

On a lightly floured surface, press the dough flat with your fingertips, leaving a half-inch border around the edge (that's your crust). Pick it up and drape it over your fists, letting gravity stretch it. Rotate slowly, letting the weight of the dough do the work. If it springs back, set it down and let it rest for 5 minutes — the gluten needs to relax.

Don't use a rolling pin. Rolling pins compress the gas bubbles that give pizza crust its characteristic airy, bubbly texture. Your hands are gentler and preserve those bubbles.

Aim for about 12 inches in diameter. It doesn't need to be a perfect circle — rustic shapes are part of the charm.

### Step 5: Top and Bake

For a home oven, preheat to the highest temperature it'll go — usually 500-550°F (260-290°C). If you have a pizza stone or steel, put it in the oven during preheating. A pizza steel is the single best investment I've made for home pizza — it transfers heat faster than stone and produces a crispier bottom crust.

No pizza stone? A preheated upside-down sheet pan works surprisingly well.

**My go-to margherita:**
- Thin layer of crushed San Marzano tomatoes (straight from the can, seasoned with salt)
- Fresh mozzarella, torn into pieces
- A drizzle of olive oil
- Fresh basil (added after baking)

Less is more with pizza toppings. Overloading makes the center soggy and prevents the crust from crisping. Two or three toppings, applied with restraint, is the sweet spot.

Slide the pizza onto the hot stone/steel and bake for 7-10 minutes. You're looking for a deeply golden, slightly charred crust with bubbles, melted and spotty cheese, and a bottom that's crisp when you tap it.

## Five Topping Combinations Worth Trying

Once you've nailed the basic margherita, branch out:

**White Pizza:** Ricotta, mozzarella, garlic, olive oil, and fresh arugula added after baking. No tomato sauce. The ricotta gets creamy and slightly browned in spots.

**Pepperoni and Hot Honey:** Classic pepperoni (the small, cup-and-char kind if you can find it) with mozzarella, finished with a drizzle of hot honey. Sweet, spicy, salty, cheesy — it hits every note.

**Mushroom and Truffle:** Sautéed mixed mushrooms, fontina cheese, and a drizzle of truffle oil after baking. This one tastes like it costs $25 at a restaurant.

**Fig and Prosciutto:** Fig jam as the base instead of tomato sauce, mozzarella, and prosciutto added after baking so it stays silky. A handful of arugula and a drizzle of balsamic glaze on top.

**BBQ Chicken:** BBQ sauce base, shredded rotisserie chicken, red onion, mozzarella, and cilantro. This is the one my kids request every Friday.

## Freezing Dough for Future Pizza Nights

After dividing and shaping into balls, you can freeze dough instead of refrigerating it. Wrap each ball tightly in plastic wrap, then place in a freezer bag. It keeps for up to 3 months.

To use: move a frozen dough ball to the fridge the night before. By the next evening, it'll be thawed and ready to shape. The texture is nearly identical to fresh — I honestly can't tell the difference most of the time.

This is why I always make the full three-ball batch even when I only need one pizza. Future me always appreciates having dough in the freezer for spontaneous pizza nights.

## Troubleshooting

**Dough tears when stretching:** It's either too cold (let it warm up longer) or the gluten is too tight (let it rest for 5-10 minutes and try again). Never force it — patience is the fix.

**Soggy center:** Too many toppings, or your oven isn't hot enough. Crank the heat as high as it goes and use fewer, drier toppings. Pre-cook watery vegetables like mushrooms or peppers before putting them on the pizza.

**Crust isn't crispy on the bottom:** You need more heat from below. A pizza steel or stone makes a huge difference. If using a sheet pan, preheat it in the oven first.

**Bland flavor:** Your dough didn't ferment long enough. Try the full 72-hour cold ferment — the difference between 24 and 72 hours is dramatic.

**Dough is too sticky to handle:** More flour on your hands and work surface. A bench scraper helps too. Some stickiness is normal with higher-hydration doughs — it's a sign of good things to come.

In our house, Friday is pizza night. I pull dough from the fridge, everyone picks their toppings, and we make individual pizzas. The kids love it because they get to build their own. I love it because it takes 20 minutes from fridge to table and costs a fraction of delivery.

There's something deeply satisfying about making pizza from scratch — watching raw dough transform into something bubbly and golden and delicious. It's one of those skills that feels impressive but is actually simple once you understand the basics.
